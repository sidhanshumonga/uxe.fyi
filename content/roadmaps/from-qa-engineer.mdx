## Who this is for

You test software. You find the bugs other people miss, you know what happens when a user clicks twice or loses signal halfway through, and you write bug reports that engineers can act on. Maybe you have started to care less about whether something passes and more about whether it feels right. That shift is the bridge to UX engineering.

Your advantage is that you already see failure modes. Much of good interface work lives at the edges: slow networks, empty data, interrupted flows, keyboard users and screen readers. Many engineers and designers only think about those cases late. You think about them first.

The gap is in building and in design judgement. Finding a problem is not the same as designing a good response to it and shipping the code. You will also need a visual eye and the confidence to propose solutions, not just report issues. This path covers both.

## How the work changes

Today you mostly work after something is built. In UX engineering, you work before and during. Instead of finding that an error state is missing, you design and build it. Instead of filing a ticket about focus order, you fix it and explain the fix in review.

Day to day, you will write product code, not just test code. You will join design reviews and ask the questions you already ask: what happens when this fails, what does a keyboard user do here, what if the list is empty. The difference is that you will also bring an answer.

Your mindset shifts from "is this broken?" to "is this good?". Something can pass every test and still confuse people. You will learn to judge an interface by how it feels to use, and to explain that judgement to designers and engineers.

Your test skills keep paying off. You can write tests for the components you build, catch regressions before review and help your team treat states like loading and error as part of the design, not an afterthought.

## Common traps

- **Staying in reporting mode.** Your value grows when you fix what you find. Start making small UI changes in real code as early as you can.
- **Treating accessibility as a checklist.** Automated tools catch some issues but not all. Learn to test with real assistive technology and understand why each rule exists.
- **Designing only for failure.** Edge cases matter, but so does the main path. Make sure your work also shows taste in the everyday flow, not just in error handling.
- **Undervaluing your background.** Your testing experience is rare in this role. Frame it as a strength rather than something to move away from.
- **Skipping visual practice.** You will need a design eye. Spend time on spacing, type and hierarchy, even if it feels less natural at first.

## How to show it

Your portfolio should show that you can turn failure modes into designed experiences. Make every piece playable: a live demo, a coded prototype or a short recording.

Follow the structure in [A UXE portfolio](/learning/a-uxe-portfolio): the interaction, the constraint and the decision. A strong QA-to-UXE portfolio might include one flow with its edge cases designed and built, one accessibility audit paired with the fixes, and one component with an explicit state model. The [breakdowns](/breakdowns), such as [undo systems](/breakdowns/undo-systems) and [autosave](/breakdowns/debounced-autosave), are good examples of how to present an interaction that has to survive failure.

In interviews, tell stories where you found a problem and then designed and shipped the answer. Explain how you decided what users should see when something went wrong. Be honest about where your design skills are still growing, and show what you are doing to build them.
