## Who this is for

You write production code for a living. You know how to break down a feature, keep a codebase healthy and ship without drama. Maybe you are the engineer who always ends up fixing the hover state, arguing about loading spinners or asking why a button jumps by two pixels. That instinct is the start of UX engineering.

Your advantage is real. Most of the role is still engineering, and you already have the hardest part to fake: you can build the thing, in the real stack, under real constraints. Designers and product managers will trust you quickly because what you show them works.

The gap is just as real. Engineering training rewards correctness, and an interface can be correct and still feel wrong. You may not yet have the words for why a layout feels cramped, or the habit of asking what the user sees during the 400ms the request takes. This path is about building that eye and that habit.

## How the work changes

The main shift is in what counts as done. Today, done probably means the tests pass and the feature behaves to spec. In UX engineering, done also means every state has been designed: the empty list, the slow network, the error that comes back halfway through, the keyboard user, the person who has turned motion off.

Day to day, you will spend more time in conversation with designers, often before a ticket exists. You will prototype in code to answer design questions, not just to build agreed designs. You will review pull requests for feel as well as structure: does the focus land in the right place, does the transition match the rest of the product, does the copy explain what went wrong.

You will also start to argue differently. "This is simpler to build" stops being enough. You will need to say why a choice is better for the person using it, and sometimes you will choose the harder build because the easier one makes the product worse.

## Common traps

- **Treating design as decoration.** Visual choices carry meaning. Spacing groups things, weight shows priority, motion shows cause and effect. Learn the reasons, not just the values.
- **Reaching for ARIA first.** Most accessibility problems are solved by the right HTML element. Extra roles on the wrong element often make things worse.
- **Over-engineering the design system.** A system with three well-documented components that people use beats a sprawling library nobody trusts. Start small and grow from real needs.
- **Polishing alone.** Craft built in isolation drifts from what the product needs. Show work early to designers and ask for crit, even when it feels unfinished.
- **Skipping the boring states.** Loading, error and empty states are where users judge your product. They are also where you can stand out fastest.

## How to show it

Your portfolio does not need to prove you can code. It needs to prove you care about how things feel and can make good calls. Pick three projects at most and make them playable: a live demo, a coded prototype or a short recording.

For each one, follow the structure in [A UXE portfolio](/learning/a-uxe-portfolio): the interaction, the constraint and the decision. A strong engineer-turned-UXE piece usually shows one accessible component with its keyboard and screen reader behaviour, one small system with tokens and docs, and one before-and-after where you improved a real screen. The [breakdowns](/breakdowns), such as [optimistic UI](/breakdowns/optimistic-ui) or [skeleton vs spinner](/breakdowns/skeleton-vs-spinner), are a good model for how to present a single interaction in depth.

In interviews, lead with judgement. Talk about a time you pushed back on a design for a user reason, or changed your own build after watching someone struggle. Be clear about what you did and what the designer did. Interviewers want to see that you can hold both sides of the work, not that you have replaced the designer.
