Most interface decisions you make in a day are applications of a small number of well-studied principles. Learning their names gives you a way to explain decisions in review instead of defending taste.

## Hick’s law

Decision time grows with the number and complexity of choices. It does not mean "fewer options are always better". It means options should be grouped, ordered and progressively revealed so the user only weighs a handful at a time.

## Fitts’s law

The time to reach a target depends on its distance and size. Edges and corners of the screen are effectively infinite targets. Small icon buttons with no padding are slow targets, however clean they look.

- Make the hit area larger than the visible icon
- Put frequent actions near where the cursor already is
- Keep destructive actions far from primary ones

Feel it for yourself: the same click, with small far targets and then large near ones.

## Mental models and signifiers

Users arrive with a model of how a thing should work, built from every other product they use. A signifier is the visible hint that tells them which model applies here: an underline, a drag handle, a chevron.

> When a design needs a tooltip to explain how to use it, the signifier is missing.
