People don't experience interfaces as code paths. They experience waiting, expectation and mistakes. A few well-established findings about perception explain a lot of what makes an interface feel good.

## Thresholds of time

Jakob Nielsen's [response time limits](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/) are a useful rule of thumb:

- **About 0.1 s** — feels instant. A pressed state or toggle should respond within this.
- **About 1 s** — the user notices the delay but keeps their train of thought. No special feedback needed beyond showing that something is happening.
- **About 10 s** — the limit of attention. Longer tasks need progress, an estimate, and a way to do something else.

## Anticipation

Users act on what they expect to happen next. Conventions (a logo that goes home, a chevron that expands) are expectations you can rely on. Breaking one should buy something significant. You can also anticipate on their behalf: prefetch the next page on hover, pre-fill what you already know.

## Perception of change

People notice change more than state. Motion, contrast and position changes draw the eye, which is why animation should be reserved for changes that matter, and why important changes elsewhere on the screen need a visible cue.

## Recovery

Errors are inevitable, so the cost of a mistake matters more than its probability. Make actions reversible, keep users' input when something fails, and put the fix next to the problem. [Undo](/breakdowns/undo-systems) is often kinder than a warning.
