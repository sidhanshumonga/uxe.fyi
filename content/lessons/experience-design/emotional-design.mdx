How a product makes people feel shapes whether they come back. Emotional design is the deliberate part of that: tone, small moments of delight, and respect for the user's state of mind.

## Three levels

In *Emotional Design* (2004), Don Norman describes three levels at which people respond to products:

- **Visceral** — the immediate reaction to how something looks and sounds
- **Behavioural** — the experience of using it: does it work, is it efficient, does it feel in control
- **Reflective** — what it means to the person afterwards: identity, memory, story

In software, the behavioural level carries most of the weight. A beautiful interface that fails at the behavioural level feels worse, not better.

## Tone follows the situation

A celebratory tone fits finishing a project; it grates on someone recovering from a failed payment. Write success messages warmly, errors calmly and plainly, and anything involving money, health or loss with care. Humour works best in low-stakes moments.

## Micro-celebration

A small reward at a real milestone (inbox zero, a first deploy, a completed course) makes the moment memorable. Keep it brief, skippable and rare. Celebrating everything makes nothing feel special.

## Respect

The strongest positive emotion a product can create is often relief: it worked, it was quick, nothing was lost. Getting the basics right is emotional design too.
